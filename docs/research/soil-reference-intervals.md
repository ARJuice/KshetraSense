# Soil reference intervals for current KshetraSense crops

**Evidence checked:** 2026-10-14
**Purpose:** Document source-backed, context-specific reference values for Apple, Tomato, and Potato. This is a research note, not a universal soil standard or crop recommendation.

## Finding

The current Crop Fit design is factor-by-factor and does not return a crop-level verdict. Its profiles provide pH ranges (Apple 6.0–7.0, Tomato 5.8–6.6, Potato 5.5–6.5) plus EC reference text; N, P, K, moisture, and soil temperature have no configured numeric ranges. The pH crop pages do not specify test method or sample depth, so a status computed from a pH number is only relative to the cited numeric interval until method and sample-layer compatibility are established. It is not a calibrated local recommendation or an overall crop-suitability result.

The cited pH and WSU soil-test ranges come from Minnesota, Pennsylvania, and Central Washington extension contexts. The report preserves those contexts and does not claim local calibration in other regions.

EC has crop-specific numeric salinity-response references for tomato and potato when it is the electrical conductivity of a saturated-paste extract averaged over the root zone (ECe). A separate Washington State University (WSU) Central Washington tree-fruit guide also gives method-specific EC, P, and K reference bands, but these are regional tree-fruit soil-test interpretations, not universal crop-fit thresholds. In the current Crop Fit implementation, an EC value is surfaced as `context_required`; no numeric threshold status is assigned. The app's generic EC in dS/m and N/P/K in mg/kg do not identify the necessary methods. No generic N, moisture, or season-long soil-temperature interval is defensible from the current measurements alone; comparisons need the missing test, depth, time, crop-stage, and management context described below.

## Current app scope

`services/zoning/app/crop_profiles.py` defines the pH bands above, FAO EC reference text, and marks N/P/K, soil temperature, and moisture as unassessed. `services/zoning/app/crop_fit.py` returns independent factor assessments, leaves EC in a context-required state with no numeric threshold classification, and explicitly avoids a crop verdict. Its request accepts optional `measurementContext` but does not require method or depth metadata. Field-scale zoning accepts pH, EC, N, P, and K, requires one common explicit sample depth, and allows `labMethod` to be absent; its units are pH, dS/m, and mg/kg respectively (`services/zoning/app/schemas.py`, `docs/TRD.md`). Field Monitoring separately stores optional temperature (°C) and moisture (%), with depth and method optional (`src/features/monitoring/types.ts`, `src/features/monitoring/soilSampleValidation.ts`). A CSV moisture unit defaults to `% gravimetric` when omitted; the historical Parma soil-humidity signal is `%RH` at 20 cm, not volumetric water content, and its environmental temperature is air temperature rather than root-zone soil temperature (`services/telemetry/data/README.md`). These representations must not be treated as equivalent merely because their displayed units look similar.

## pH: supported as a contextual screen

| Crop | Source interval | Source context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Apple | pH 6.0–7.0 | UMN Extension home-orchard guidance for Minnesota. It also says apple can grow in most soils only if standing water is absent; that drainage condition is separate from pH. [UMN Extension: Growing apples](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/gardening-in-minnesota/growing-apples) |
| Tomato | pH 5.8–6.6 | Penn State commercial tomato production guidance; lime is applied to reach the band according to soil-test recommendations. [Penn State Extension: Tomato production](https://extension.psu.edu/tomato-production) |
| Potato | pH 5.5–6.5 | Penn State potato production guidance; it recommends annual soil testing and says fertility management also depends on crop use, cultivar, season length, and manure history. [Penn State Extension: Potato production](https://extension.psu.edu/potato-production) |

These are the same source values currently configured in Crop Fit, not one harmonized global standard. UMN's Minnesota home-garden potato guidance, for example, calls pH 6.0–6.5 ideal while saying potatoes tolerate soil as low as pH 5.0. That illustrates the difference between a preferred target and a tolerance limit, and why sources must remain visible. [UMN Extension: Growing potatoes](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/gardening-in-minnesota/growing-potatoes)

pH is unitless, but the method still matters. The crop-specific pages do not state their extraction ratio or sampling depth. Penn State's soil-testing page says its lab reports water pH; UMN describes its water-pH test as mixing equal weights of soil and water. USDA-NRCS lists 1:1 water pH and 1:2 0.01 M CaCl₂ pH as distinct commonly used determinations. Therefore, retain the reported pH method (including extractant and soil:solution ratio) rather than silently equating lab, field-probe, water, and salt-solution results. [Penn State soil testing](https://extension.psu.edu/soil-testing), [UMN soil-test pH method](https://blog-crop-news.extension.umn.edu/2024/03/soil-test-ph-and-liming-common.html), [USDA-NRCS Soil Survey Laboratory Methods Manual](https://nrcs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/2022-10/SSIR45.pdf)

As a regional cross-check, WSU's Central Washington tree-fruit guide lists pH 6.0–7.5 as optimal, with the method printed in its table as `1:1 /CaCl`. That source notation is ambiguous about the exact protocol, so it should be retained verbatim rather than interpreted as a fully specified method. This is a tree-fruit recommendation for that region, distinct from the UMN Minnesota home-orchard Apple band above. [WSU Tree Fruit: Soil fertility and plant nutrition in cropping orchards in Central Washington](https://treefruit.wsu.edu/orchard-management/soils-nutrition/fruit-tree-nutrition/)

Sampling depth is also source-specific. Penn State describes 6–8 in (15–20 cm) garden samples and 6–7 in (15–18 cm) samples for cultivated crop fields; the apple, tomato, and potato pH pages themselves do not specify a depth. These Pennsylvania sampling depths are examples of the context attached to those soil-test programs, not a universal depth to impose on other locations. [Penn State soil testing](https://extension.psu.edu/soil-testing), [Penn State agronomic soil sampling](https://extension.psu.edu/soil-sampling)

For orchards, WSU's 2026 sampling guide says most tree-fruit feeder roots are in the upper 2 ft, but rootstock and site limitations can shift them; it recommends checking root depth with soil pits and sampling relevant layers rather than assuming a universal depth. It also recommends consistent annual timing for trend comparisons and notes spring sampling can better represent mobile nutrients such as N. Thus an orchard sample layer should be linked to observed active roots, not copied from a vegetable-field sample protocol. [WSU Tree Fruit: Soil sampling for tree fruit orchards](https://treefruit.wsu.edu/soil-sampling-for-tree-fruit-orchards/)

## EC: yield-response comparisons require root-zone ECe

FAO-56 Table 23 reports salinity-yield thresholds in dS/m of ECe, defined as the mean electrical conductivity of a saturated-paste extract for the crop root zone. It describes the threshold as the point where yield first falls below full yield potential and gives a slope beyond that threshold:

| Crop | Yield basis | ECe threshold where yield reduction begins | Slope above threshold |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Tomato | Yield-response table row “Tomato” | 0.9–2.5 dS/m | 9.0% per dS/m |
| Potato | Yield-response table row “Potato” | 1.7 dS/m | 12.0% per dS/m |
| Apple | “Deciduous orchard—Apples”; sensitive rating | No numeric threshold reported in this table | No numeric slope reported |

Source: [FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56, Chapter 8, Table 23](https://www.fao.org/4/X0490E/x0490e0e.htm). ECe is reported at 25°C. The table compiles values from FAO Irrigation and Drainage Papers 29 and 48; it is a yield-response model under optimum management, not a general crop-fit score. FAO says the tabulated threshold/slope were determined primarily in research experiments where soil water was maintained near field capacity; values are guidance and can vary with climate, soil, and cultural practices. It also notes crops are often less tolerant at germination/seedling stage. For woody crops such as apple, Na⁺/Cl⁻ toxicity and cultivar/rootstock differences complicate a total-salinity threshold; FAO-56 and the woody-crop Annex classify apple as sensitive but supply no numeric threshold or slope.

FAO's separate Annex 1 compilation lists standard tomato fruit yield at ECe 2.5 dS/m with a 9.9%/dS/m slope, cherry tomato fruit yield at 1.7 dS/m with a 9.1%/dS/m slope, and potato tuber yield at 1.7 dS/m and 12%/dS/m. These separate values explain why a single tomato cutoff should not be detached from source/cultivar context: FAO-56 reports a 0.9–2.5 threshold range across cited data, whereas Annex 1 gives separate standard- and cherry-tomato rows. Annex 1 says much of its data reflect commercial practices but are not standardized across crops and apply to fairly uniform salinity from late seedling to maturity; it flags seedling-stage sensitivity, gypsiferous soils, and woody-crop ion/rootstock variation. [FAO Annex 1, Crop salt tolerance data](https://www.fao.org/4/Y4263E/y4263e0e.htm)

The absence of an Apple yield-response threshold in the FAO tables does not mean no numeric tree-fruit EC guidance exists. WSU's Central Washington tree-fruit table reports an “optimal” saturated-paste EC below 2.6 mmhos/cm and “excessive” above 4; it separately lists a soil-solution EC row (`1:2.5 or 1:1`) with optimal below 0.5 and excessive above 1, with `1:1` in its method column. The solution-extract row's exact protocol is not fully clear from the table, so preserve the source label. These are regional tree-fruit bands, not an Apple-specific yield threshold/slope and not interchangeable with FAO's ECe response function. [WSU tree-fruit soil test ranges](https://treefruit.wsu.edu/orchard-management/soils-nutrition/fruit-tree-nutrition/)

An app value recorded simply as `EC: dS/m` cannot be compared to those ECe values until the method is known. USDA-NRCS notes that 1:1, 1:2, and 1:5 soil:water extracts are not directly comparable because of dilution, and that apparent field EC is also affected by soil texture, water content, bulk density, organic matter, clay mineralogy, and temperature. ECe is the salinity metric used by the FAO crop-tolerance table. A unit conversion (for example, µS/cm to dS/m) does not convert EC methods. [USDA-NRCS Field Book for Describing and Sampling Soils, v4.0](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/2025-01/Field-Book-for-Describing-and-Sampling-Soils-v4.pdf)

Accordingly, a conditional FAO ECe comparison would need the saturated-paste extraction method, unit, root-zone sampling interval, crop and stage, and relevant salt/soil context. A generic sensor, EC₁:₁, EC₁:₅, irrigation-water EC, or the Parma trial's µS/cm sensor value is not a substitute for root-zone ECe. For WSU's tree-fruit bands, the sample must instead match the specific paste or source-labeled solution method and Central Washington production context; a unit conversion alone cannot harmonize extraction methods.

## N, P, and K: mg/kg alone is not an interpretable crop interval

The app labels N, P, and K in mg/kg, but does not require the analyte form or extraction method for a field sample. Soil-test concentrations are method-defined indices of an extractable fraction, not necessarily the total nutrient present or a shared unit-independent measure of plant availability.

There is a useful conditional counterexample to “no intervals exist”: WSU publishes the following Central Washington tree-fruit soil-test bands, with `ppm = mg/kg` per its note. The page describes the table as tree-fruit guidance (not an Apple-only calibration), and it says the same test method and units must be used. It does not give one fixed sample depth in the range table; its separate orchard sampling guide says to sample according to actual feeder-root depth and site layers.

| Soil test | WSU tree-fruit optimal band | Method shown by WSU | Scope caveat |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| P-Olsen | 15–40 mg/kg; low <10; excessive >50 | NaHCO₃; WSU recommends P-Olsen for mildly acid to alkaline eastern-Washington soils | Central Washington/eastern-Washington tree-fruit interpretation |
| K | 150–250 mg/kg; low <120; excessive >300 | NH₄OAc | Same regional tree-fruit interpretation |

[WSU Central Washington tree-fruit fertility guide](https://treefruit.wsu.edu/orchard-management/soils-nutrition/fruit-tree-nutrition/), [WSU orchard sampling guide](https://treefruit.wsu.edu/soil-sampling-for-tree-fruit-orchards/)

This source does not give a numeric soil-N sufficiency band: it says nitrate and ammonium are mobile and variable, so a soil result reflects sampling time and does not correlate well with supply; it describes root-zone organic matter as a better but still uncertain N-supply indicator because mineralization depends on moisture, temperature, and microbiome. WSU separately reports Apple leaf-tissue targets for recently mature leaves sampled in the more stable mid-July to mid-August window: N 1.7–2.5%, P 0.15–0.30%, and K 1.2–1.9% of dry weight. These are a different sample matrix and cannot be converted into soil mg/kg targets. [WSU leaf-tissue standards](https://treefruit.wsu.edu/orchard-management/soils-nutrition/fruit-tree-nutrition/)

Examples from official soil-test guidance show why a bare mg/kg value is insufficient:

- Penn State reports water pH and Mehlich-3 (ICP) P and K; its fertilizer recommendations are tied to the crop identified for that soil-test submission. [Penn State soil testing](https://extension.psu.edu/soil-testing)
- USDA-NRCS explains that P-test choice depends on soil chemistry: Bray 1/Mehlich methods are used for acidic soils, while Olsen is recommended for alkaline/calcareous soils; multiple methods can be applicable in intermediate pH soils. A P value from one extractant is not automatically equivalent to another. [USDA-NRCS: Testing methods for phosphorus](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/conservation-basics/soil/testing-methods-for-phosphorus-and-organic-matter)
- UMN describes nitrate-N reports as concentration by sampled depth increment, with ppm converted to pounds per acre according to the depth sampled. A generic “nitrogen mg/kg” field does not say whether it is nitrate-N, ammonium-N, total N, or another test. [UMN Extension: Understanding the soil test report](https://extension.umn.edu/agriculture/crop-production/nutrient-management-for-minnesota-crops/understanding-the-soil-test-report)
- Penn State's potato guidance notes that excess N delays maturity and excess K can hinder Mg uptake and reduce tuber firmness/specific gravity. Its tomato and potato production pages direct fertilizer decisions to annual soil-test recommendations rather than a universal N/P/K concentration band. [Penn State potato production](https://extension.psu.edu/potato-production), [Penn State tomato production](https://extension.psu.edu/tomato-production)

The WSU values can support a comparison only when the crop context, regional calibration, analyte, extractant/protocol, reporting basis, sample layer, and timing are compatible. A generic N/P/K value cannot be compared until these details and local crop calibration, crop/use, yield target, and recent fertilizer/manure history are known. If those are absent, keep the values visible and mark the crop factor unassessed, as the current Crop Fit profile does. In particular, WSU's tree-fruit soil P/K bands do not justify assigning those bands to tomato or potato, or to orchards outside the source context.

## Moisture: compare root-zone water status, not a lone percent

FAO-56 defines total available water as `TAW = 1000(qFC − qWP) Zr`, where field-capacity (`qFC`) and wilting-point (`qWP`) water contents are volumetric (m³/m³) and `Zr` is effective rooting depth in metres; readily available water is `RAW = p × TAW`. In its table for an evapotranspiration rate near 5 mm/day, tomato has a maximum effective root-depth range of 0.7–1.5 m and depletion fraction `p = 0.40`; potato has 0.4–0.6 m and `p = 0.35`; apples/cherries/pears are grouped at 1.0–2.0 m and `p = 0.50`. These are scheduling/model parameters, not universal moisture percentages or pre-plant suitability cutoffs. FAO notes root depth is restricted by soil layering and other conditions, and `p` changes with crop evapotranspiration and soil texture. [FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56, Chapter 8](https://www.fao.org/4/X0490E/x0490e0e.htm)

As a separate regional vegetable-management example, UMN Extension says most crops generally do not experience stress until about 40–60% of available water-holding capacity (AWC) has been removed; it gives broader early-season allowable deficits (up to 60–65% of plant-available water for most crops) and mid-season limits (30–40%). These are generalized irrigation-management guidelines, not tomato- or potato-specific sensor-percent targets; the useful comparison is depletion relative to local AWC and crop stage, not percent moisture alone. [UMN Extension: Irrigation strategies for vegetables](https://extension.umn.edu/agriculture/specialty-crops/vegetable-farming/irrigation-strategies-for-vegetables)

To assess water status, the measurement needs a declared metric (volumetric content, gravimetric content, matric potential, or a named sensor signal), calibration, date/time, depth(s) representing the active root zone, soil water-holding context (including field capacity and wilting point for a water-balance calculation), and crop stage/ET conditions. The app's optional moisture percent, default `% gravimetric` import unit, and historical `%RH` sensor signal cannot be compared as though all were volumetric water content.

## Soil temperature: only stage- and depth-specific examples

Penn State says commercial tomato transplants should wait until soil temperature at 3 in (7.6 cm) reaches 60°F (15.6°C). For potatoes, Penn State home-garden guidance says to plant when soil warms to 45°F (7.2°C), while a University of Maine potato resource reports a 50°F (10°C) planting reference measured at 6 in (15 cm) in Presque Isle, Maine, to reduce seed-decay risk. These are planting conditions from different regional contexts, not whole-season crop-fit intervals. [Penn State tomato production](https://extension.psu.edu/tomato-production), [Penn State potatoes in the garden](https://extension.psu.edu/potatoes-in-the-garden-and-the-kitchen), [University of Maine potato soil temperatures](https://extension.umaine.edu/potatoes/weather/soil-temps-six-inches-presque-isle/)

The app records temperature in °C but does not consistently require reading depth, measurement time/averaging window, or crop stage. Without those fields, a temperature value cannot be compared to the stage-specific examples above. No apple root-zone temperature interval is configured or supported by the sources reviewed here.

## Minimum context for a future comparison

Before displaying a numeric crop-reference comparison, preserve at least:

- the crop and relevant production context (region, cultivar/rootstock where relevant, home garden vs commercial field, and growth stage/use);
- the exact parameter/analyte, unit, lab or sensor method, and extraction/dilution ratio where relevant;
- the sample depth or root-zone interval and collection time/date;
- the local interpretation basis, including soil texture/chemistry, expected yield and nutrient-management context where the source requires it.

Current conclusions should remain factor-specific. The named pH bands can be shown as source context, but a definitive within/outside label requires method and sample-layer compatibility that the crop-profile pages do not supply. ECe may be compared to the FAO tomato/potato salinity-response references only after extraction method and root-zone context match; the current API deliberately assigns no EC threshold state to a single-depth estimate. WSU P/K/EC comparisons are possible only for compatible regional tree-fruit samples and source-listed methods. Otherwise N, P, K, moisture, and temperature remain visible but **not assessed**; generic EC likewise remains context-required. These are comparison limits inferred from the cited method, scope, and sampling requirements; none of the comparisons alone establishes overall suitability, yield, or probability of cultivation.
